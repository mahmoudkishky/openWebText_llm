Marie Curie was a physicist and chemist. She was born Maria Salomea Sklodowska on November 7, 1867, in Warsaw, which was then part of the Russian Empire. She was Polish by birth and did most of her work in France. She died on July 4, 1934, of aplastic anaemia, an illness caused by her long exposure to radiation.

In 1891 Marie moved to Paris to study at the Sorbonne, which is also called the University of Paris. She studied physics and mathematics there. She married a physicist named Pierre Curie, and the two scientists worked together.

Marie studied radioactivity, building on the earlier research of Henri Becquerel. In 1898 she and Pierre discovered two new elements, polonium and radium. She named polonium after Poland, her native country. She also developed methods to separate radium from other materials so that radioactive substances could be studied.

In 1903 Marie received the Nobel Prize in Physics. She shared it with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel for their work on radiation. She was the first woman ever to win a Nobel Prize. In 1911 she won a second Nobel Prize, this time in Chemistry, for discovering polonium and radium and for isolating radium. She was the first person to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.

Pierre died in 1906, and Marie took over his teaching job at the University of Paris. She became the first woman to be a professor there.

During World War I, Marie helped wounded soldiers. She developed mobile X-ray units, which people nicknamed the little Curies, and took them close to the front lines. She also helped set up research institutes for radioactive elements in Paris and in Warsaw.
