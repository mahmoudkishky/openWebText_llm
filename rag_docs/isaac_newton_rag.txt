Isaac Newton was an English mathematician and physicist. He was born in 1642 in the small village of Woolsthorpe in Lincolnshire, England. He died in London in 1727 at the age of eighty-four, and he was buried in Westminster Abbey.

Newton studied at Trinity College at the University of Cambridge. In 1665 a plague closed the university, so he went home to Woolsthorpe for about two years. During this time he worked out many of his key ideas in mathematics and optics. Later he became the Lucasian Professor of Mathematics at Cambridge.

Newton developed the three laws of motion and the law of universal gravitation. His laws showed that the same rules explain how objects fall on Earth and how the planets move in the sky. He also invented a kind of calculus that he called the method of fluxions. The German mathematician Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed calculus independently.

Newton also studied light. He used a glass prism to show that white light is made of many colors. He built one of the first practical reflecting telescopes, which used a mirror instead of a lens.

Newton published his most famous book, the Principia, in 1687. Its full title is Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica. It explained the laws of motion and gravity. In 1704 he published a second book, called Opticks, about his experiments with light and color.

Newton also served the public. He worked at the Royal Mint, first as its warden and later as its master, where he helped reform the currency and catch counterfeiters. In 1703 he was elected president of the Royal Society. In 1705 he was made a knight.
